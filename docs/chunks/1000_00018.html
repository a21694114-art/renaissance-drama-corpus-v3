Doctor! art a Parisian, a Paduan, or a Leaden Doctor? How many and be true to us hast thou killed the last Spring, will it puzzle thy arithmetic, my precious Rectifier of nature, the wrong way,— faith Thou must excuse me Jack that I cannot condole With thee, by this whey beard of Esculapius, I dare Not endanger myself with so much melancholy lest I fall into a relapse— whom have we here? 'tis reported that Master Fowler is sick and keeps his chamber, I hope he's within. Noble Sir. Fair Lady. How fare you Sir? The better to see you here. Upon the entrance of this Gentlewoman, I find your grief much altered. Upon mine? Yes, and by that I dare presume to say you are the cause of his distemper. I Sir? A cunning Doctor For I observed so soon as his searching eye had fastened on her, his labouring pulse that through his Fever did, before stick hard, and frequent now exceeds in both these differences and this Galen himself found true upon a woman, that had doted upon a Fencer. I? she did long for other bout then, Give us leave pray. A very pretty fellow, Well skilled i'th' pulse. You know my disease too d'ee not? will not my complexion give you the hint on't You are not very well. How Sir? By your favour you will come to't. To what? To a burning Fever— is there not one woman in the world. I think there is, and too much of that, what then what conclude you? Nothing but Syrup of Violet, would comfort you going to bed. Violet? Has given't you. It does me good Lady to feel you by— the hand. Would it were in my power to recover you. The Doctor I thank him has taken pains with me, but he says— nothing will do me good. Nothing? But that, which is another sickness to reveal. Pray Sir acquaint me. I know you love me. I have a great mind and 'twere but for two or 3. Minutes, to have a maid warm my bed. That may be done. With her body— else 'twill do me no good the Doctor says— to put life in some of my limbs, a little virgin warmth would do't. You have a burning Fever. But now and then I have such cold fits again— and 'tis the doctor's opinion— a very learned man. A learned Pander. He's at it. Doctor. Again passionate. Why, I may love her name without offence, to you, why did he waken my remembrance I had forgotten her. Think upon her scorn then. I ha' done, and till I hear her name again I will throw this dulness off. let's choose another subject How closely they consult, the Doctor is in a fit of counsel I suspect some juggling— he comes of, I'll gauge a limb this fellow's an impostor. Is there not much danger in him?
