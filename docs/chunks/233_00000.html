Text (EarlyPrint-regularized spelling)

WHY looks my Lord like to a troubled sky, When heavens bright shine, is shadowed with a fog: O'late we ran the deer and through the lands Stripped with our Nags the lofty frolic Bucks, That scudded fore the teasers like the wind, ne'er was the deer of merry Fressingfield, So lustily pulled down by jolly mates, Nor shared the Farmers such fat venison, So frankly dealt this hundred years before: Nor have I seen my Lord more frolic in the chase, And now changed to a melancholy dump. After the Prince got to the keeper's lodge And had been jocund in the house a while: Tossing of Ale and milk in country cans, Whether it was the country's sweet content, Or else the bonny Damsel filled us drink That seemed so stately in her stammel red: Or that a qualm did cross his stomach then, But straight he fell into his passions. Sirrah Raphe, what say you to your master, Shall he thus all amort live malcontent? Hearest thou Ned? nay look if he will speak to me. What sayst thou to me, Fool? I pray thee tell me Ned, art thou in love with the keeper's daughter? How if I be, what then? Why then sirrah, I'll teach thee how to deceive Love. How Raphe. Marry sirrah Ned, thou shalt put on my cap, and my coat, and my dagger, and I will put on thy clothes, and thy sword, and so thou shalt be my fool. And what of this? Why so thou shalt beguile Love, for Love is such a proud scab, that he will never meddle with fools nor children. Is not Ralph's counsel good, Ned. Tell me Ned Lacie, didst thou mark the maid, How lively in her country weeds she looked? A bonnier wench all Suffolk cannot yield, All Suffolk, nay all England holds none such. Sirrah, Will Ermsby, Ned is deceived. Why Raphe? He says all England hath no such, and I say, and I'll stand to it, there is one better in Warwickshire. How provest thou that Raphe? Why is the Abbot a learned man, and hath he read many books, and thinkest thou he hath not more learning than thou to choose a bonny wench, yes warrant I thee by his whole Grammar. A good reason Raphe. I tell thee Lacie, that her sparkling eyes Do lighten forth sweet love's alluring fire: And in her tresses she doth fold the looks Of such a gaze upon her golden hair, Her bashful white mixed with the morning's red, Luna doth boast upon her lovely cheeks, Her front is beauty's table where's she paints The glories of her gorgeous excellence: Her teeth are shelves of precious Margarites, Richly enclosed with ruddy coral cleves. Tush Lacie, she is beauties overmatch, If thou survey'st her curious imagery.
