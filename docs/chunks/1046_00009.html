I say no, Unless Charles had a soul to understand it, Can he manage six thousand Crowns a year Out of the metaphysics? or can all His learned Astronomy look to my Vineyards? Can the drunken old Poets make up my Vines? (I know they can drink ''em) or your excellent humanists Sell 'em the Merchants for my best advantage? Can History cut my hay, or get my Corn in? And can Geometry vent it in the market? Shall I have my sheep kept with a Iacobs staff now? I wonder you will magnify this mad man, You that are old and should understand. Should, sayst thou, Thou monstrous piece of ignorance in office! Thou that hast no more knowledge than thy Clerk infuses, Thy dapper Clerk larded with ends of Latin, And he no more than custom of offences; Thou unreprieveable Dunce! that thy formal bandstrings, Thy ring nor pomander cannot expiate for, Dost thou tell me I should? I'll pose thy Worship In thine own Library and Almanac, Which thou art daily poring on to pick out Days of iniquity to cozen fools in, And full Moons to cut Cattle; dost thou taint me, That have run over story, Poetry, Humanity? As a cold nipping shadow Does o'er the ears of Corn, and leave 'em blasted, Put up your anger, what I'll do I'll do. Thou shalt not do. I will. Thou art an Ass then, A dull old tedious Ass, th' art ten times worse And of less credit than Dunce Hollingshead The Englishman, that writes of snows and Sheriffs. Well take your pleasure, here's one I must talk with. Good day Sir. Fair to you Sir. May I speak w' ye. With all my heart, I was waiting on your goodness. Good morrow Monsieur Miramont. O sweet Sir, Keep your good morrow to cool your worship's pottage, A couple of the world's fools met together To raise up dirt and dunghills. Are they drawn? They shall be ready Sir, within these two hours, And Charles set his hand. 'Tis necessary, For he being a joint purchaser, though your state Was got by your own industry, unless He seal to the Conveyance, it can be Of no validity. He shall be ready, And do it willingly. He shall be hanged first. I hope your daughter likes. She loves him well Sir, Young Eustace is a bait to catch a woman, A budding sprightly fellow, y'are resolved then, That all shall pass from Charles. All all, he's nothing, A bunch of books shall be his patrimony, And more than he can manage too. Will your brother Pass over his land too, to your son Eustace? You know he has no heir. He will be flayed first. And horse-collars made of 's skin! Let him alone, A wilful man; my state shall serve the turn, Sir. And how does your Daughter? Ready for the hour, And like a blushing Rose that stays the pulling.
