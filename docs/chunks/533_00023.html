I'll ha''t i'faith, here's enough in it to serve for my letters as long as I live; I'll keep it to breed on as 'twere: But I much wonder you could make her write. Indeed there were some words belonged to that. How strong an influence works in well placed words, And yet there must be a prepared love, To give those words so mighty a command, Or 'twere impossible they should move so much: And will you tell me true? In any thing. Does not this Lady love you? Love me? why yes; I think she does not hate me. Nay but i'faith, does she not love you dearly? No I protest. Nor have you never kissed her? Kissed her, that's nothing. But you know my meaning: Have you not been, as one would say, afore me? Not I, I swear. O, you're too true to tell. Nay be my troth, she has, I must confess, Used me with good respect, and nobly still, But for such matters. Very little more, Would make him take her maidenhead upon him: Well friend, I rest yet in a little doubt, This was not hers. 'twas by that light that shines, And I'll go fetch her to you to confirm it. O passing friend. But when she comes, in any case be bold, And come upon her with some pleasing thing, To show you're pleased: however she behaves her, As for example; if she turn her back, Use you that action you would do before, And court her thus; Lady, your back part is as fair to me, as is your forepart. 'twill be most pleasing. ay, for if you love One part above another, 'tis a sign You like not all alike, and the worst part About your Mistress, you must think as fair, As sweet, and dainty, as the very best, So much, for so much, and considering too, Each several limb and member in his kind. As a man should. True, will you think of this? I hope I shall. But if she chance to laugh, You must not lose your countenance, but devise Some speech to show you pleased, even being laughed at. ay, but what speech? God's precious man! do something of yourself? But I'll devise a speech. Inspire him folly. Or 'tis no matter, be but bold enough, And laugh when she laughs, and it is enough: I'll fetch her to you. Now was there ever such a demi-lance, To bear a man so clear through thick and thin? Or hark you sir, if she should steal a laughter Under her fan, thus you may say, sweet Lady, If you will laugh and lie down, I am pleased. And so I were by heaven; how know you that? 'slid man, I'll hit your very thoughts in these things.
