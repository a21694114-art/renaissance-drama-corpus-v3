Text (EarlyPrint-regularized spelling)

WHY looks my lord like to a troubled sky, When heavens bright shine, is shadowed with a fog: A late we ran the dear and through the Lawns Stripped with our nags the lofty frolic bucks, That scudded fore the teasers like the wind, Near was the Dear of merry Fresingfield, So lustily pulled down by jolly mates, Nor shared the Farmers such fat venison, So frankly dealt this hundred years before: Nor have I seen my lord more frolic in the chase, And now changed to a melancholy dump. After the Prince got to the keepers lodge And had been jocund in the house a while: Tossing of ale and milk in country cans, Whether it was the country's sweet content: Or else the bonny damsel filled us drink That seemed so stately in her stammel red: Or that a qualm did cross his stomach then, But strait he fell into his passions. Sirrah Ralph, what say you to your master, Shall he thus all amort live malcontent. Heerest thou Ned, nay look if he will speak to me. What sayest thou to me fool? I pray thee tell me Ned, art thou in love with the keeper's daughter? How if I be, what then? Why then sirrah I'll teach thee how to deceive love. How Ralph. Marry sirrah Ned, thou shalt put on my cap, and my coat, and my dagger, and I will put on thy clothes, and thy sword, and so thou shalt be my fool. And what of this? Why so thou shalt beguile Love, for Love is such a proud scab, that he will never meddle with fools nor children, Is not Ralph's counsel good Ned. Tell me Ned Lacie, didst thou mark the maid, How lively in her country weeds she looked: A bonnier wench all Suffolk cannot yield, All Suffolk, nay all England holds none such. Sirrah, Will Ermsby, Ned is deceived. Why Ralph? He says all England hath no such, and I say, and I'll stand to it, there is one better in Warwickshire. How proovest thou that Ralph? Why is not the Abbot a learned man, and hath red many books, and thinkest thou he hath not more learning than thou to choose a bonny wench, yes I warrant thee by his whole grammar. A good reason Ralph. I tell the Lacie, that hersparkling eyes, Do lighten forth sweet loves alluring fire: And in her tresses she doth fold the looks Of such as gaze upon her golden hair, Her bashful white mixed with the morning's red, Luna doth boast upon her lovely cheeks, Her front is beauty's table where she paints, The glories of her gorgeous excellence: Her teeth are shelves of precious Margarites, Richly enclosed with ruddy coral cleves. Tush Lacie, she is beauty's overmatch, If thou survey'st her curious imagery.
