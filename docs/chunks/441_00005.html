Who's that? Lord Sancto Danila? he's entered on the other side the Castle, With all his power. Not yet full ten, my mind presageth good, Antwerp ere night, shall bathe herself in blood. Monsieur champaign, great Antwerp's Governor, Will you refuse these fair and fresh supplies? Sent from the Prince of Orange and the States, Under our Conduct for your cities guard? Lord marquess Hawrye, we reject them not, Nor yet neglect the love of that great Prince, And our kind friend the Co-united States: But since we have no need of such a power, Why should we pester Antwerp with such troops, To spend the victuals of the Citizens, Which we can scarcely compass now for gilt. The Prince & States will furnish ye with store, To feed the Army and relieve the Town. It may be so. And it may not be so. Outwords and honours be engaged for it. Under correction my Lord Governor, The Marks and Count Egmont's noble words, (Although the Prince of Orange and the States Should be forgetful) were a pawn of worth. What says the Colonel of the Almains to it? This is Van Ends opinion my good Lord, That the rich promise of such noble Peers, As marquess Hanurie and Count Egmont are, Is pawn enough for all the cities wealth. The Almain lies, wealth is worth more than words. I way their promise with my sound belief, And tie my thoughts to their assured trust, Yet are there many reasons of import, To bar your Army's entrance to this town. The grand objection is decided. True. The lesser than are easily refeled. Suppose the Prince & States do Victual them, Yet their disorder in our Civil streets, May be pernicious, and breed mutiny. By this supposal you enfeeble us, And tax our worth with indiscretion, As though our skill and our Authority, Stood upon bases of weak discipline. We said not so, and yet their ryotings, May taint our wives and jeopardise our wealth. In silence be it my Lord, you need them not. All riots shall be death by martial law, And all commanders, shall be vigilant Over their troops, that order may he kept. My Lords of Egmount and of Hauurye, What are your numbers? just 3000, foot, One thousand horse, 800. at the least. An honourable tender of true friends, To send such aid for safeguard of your City. 'twill shake our bags too much to pay so many. At whose expense shall all this army rest? Some part yourselves, some part the Prince will pay. six hundred Almains are our garrison, A guard sufficient to defend our walls, And men enough, because we need no more. And they too many to be paid by us. O may these slaves refuse this succour sent, Their misery shall bring their misery.
