Text (EarlyPrint-regularized spelling)

i'faith you shan't go yet; what have you in your pack? What's that to you? prithee, Musica, tell me, what thou hast in thy pack? Why, because you speak kindly now, and entreat me, I'll show you. Hay, brave! what's here? Morris-bells? And waistcoats, and napkins? Why, how cam'st thou by them? Why, thus: my Mistress had been ill a good while, and because I tended her very carefully; she gave me leave to recreate myself today; and i'faith I light on merry company, where they used these jinglers: and when they had done, they prayed me to carry them home with this bottle of drink. Faith, and there were enough, we'd dance. Enough? now I think on't, there's just enough, there's six pair. Faith we'll to it then, but what wouldst thou do, Musica? Why, I'll play the maid Marian. A match, a match: dress, dress, we'll have brave jingling. I can't dance. Nay, prithee be not sullen, good Melancholico. If I do, I'll wear no bells. Why then lay one pair aside. But I won't dance now. Why, Melancholico? I won't dance, unless I have one of the wrought waistcoats. Why, now they have put them on. I care not, I won't dance else. Come prithee, Cheiromantes, slip off thine again and change with him; Melancholico must have his sullen humours. So, now we want nothing but the Tabor we talked of: but 'tis no matter, since he does not come, we'll sing, and so make music to ourselves. Who can tune the morris best? Oh, here they are both, here they are both. O my arm, my arm! O my shin! Ah, murrain on him; who the devil's this? I have hurt my breast. O the side of my face! A rope on you, must you throw me quite down? Prithee dance the morris quietly with us: up, up, ho, and we'll dance. A murrain go with you— Musica, who played in the hobby-horse? No, I must not tell. Come then, we'll go now to Barleibreak. ay but there's one odd: what shall he do? sit out every time? Yes faith, and give a reason of the other three couples meeting. Agreed: run. A murrain on't, must I be the first man must sit out? nothing angers me but that. Nay Choler, thou'lt fret and chafe now— Come Choler, your reasons. Why, thou and Musica are met together— because— Sanguine folks are most fit for Music and sports. Physiognomus and Chieromantes met, because they feared we would have suspected they would have picked our pockets, if they had joined with any of us— We thank you Choler, we shall be even with you, and't come to our turn. Melancholico, and Phlegmatico joined; because one's too dry; and the other's too moist: and so they'll serve for Medicines one for another: come run again: I'll be sure to catch somebody this time.
