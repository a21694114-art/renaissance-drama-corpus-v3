Thanks Landoys, and here Earl Richmond's vows, If their kind promises take but effect, That as they have promised I be made King, I will so deal in governing the state, Which now lies like a savage sheltered grove, Where brambles, briers, and thorns, overgrow those sprigs, Which if they might but spring to their effect, And not be crossed so by their contraries, Making them subject to these outrages, Would prove such members of the Commonweal, That England should in them be honoured, As much as ever was the Roman state, When it was governed by the Counsels rule, And I will draw my sword brave countrymen, And never leave to follow my resolve, Till I have mowed those brambles, briers and thorns That hinder those that long to do us good. Why we have scaped the dangeroust brunt of all, Which was his garrison at Milford Haven, Shall we dismay, or daunt our friends to come? Because he took the Duke of Buckingham? No worthy friends, and loving countrymen, Oxford did never bear so base a mind, He will not wink at murders secretly put up, Nor suffer upstarts to enjoy our rights, Nor live in England under an usurping king, And this is Oxford's resolution. But Blunt, look whose's that knocks. My Lord, 'tis a messenger from the mother Queen, And the Lady Stanley your mother, with letters. Admit him straight, now shall we hear some news. Long live Earl Richmond. The mother Queen doth greet your honour. Welcome my friend, how fares our mother & the rest? In health my Lord, and glad to hear of your arrival safe. My friend, my mother hath written to me of certain that are coming in our aid, the report of whose names are referred to thee to deliver. First, theirs the Lord Talbot, the Earl of Shrevesbury son and heir, with a brave band of his own. There is also the Lord Fitz Harbart, the Earl of Pembroke's son and heir. Of the Gentlemen of the Welsh, there is sir prize up Thomas, and sir Thomas up Richard, & sir Owen Williams, brave gentlemen my Lord. These are the chief. Are these the full number of all that come? Only two more my Lord, which I have left unnamed, the one is sir Thomas Denis a Western gentleman, and joined with him one Arnoll Butler, a great many are willing, but dares not as yet. Doth Arnoll Butler come, I can hardly brook his treachery, for he it was that wrought my disgrace with the King. Well my Lord, we are now to strengthen ourselves with friends, and not to reap up old quarrels, say that Arnoll Butler did injury you in the time of peace, the mends is twice made, if he stand with you in the time of wars.
