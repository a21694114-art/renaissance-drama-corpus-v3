I shall deliver so: thanks to your Majesty. My Liege, I hope they will not come upon us now. We are in God's hand brother, not in theirs: To night we will encamp beyond the bridge, And on to morrow bid them march away. Tut I have the best armour in the world. You have an excellent armour, But let my horse have his due. Now you talk of a horse, I have a steed like the Palfrey of the sun, nothing but pure air and fire, And hath none of this dull element of earth within him. He is of the colour of the Nutmeg. And of the heat, a the Ginger. Turn all the sands into eloquent tongues, And my horse is argument for them all: I once writ a Sonnet in the praise of my horse, And began thus. Wonder of nature. I have heard a Sonnet begin so, In the praise of ones Mistress. Why then did they imitate that Which I writ in praise of my horse, For my horse is my mistress. Ma foy the other day, me thought Your mistress shook you shrewdly. I bearing me. I tell thee Lord Constable, My mistress wears her own hair. I could make as good a boast of that, If I had had a sow to my mistress. Tut thou wilt make use of any thing. Yet I do not use my horse for my mistress. Will it never be morning? He ride too morrow a nule, And my way shallbe paved with English faces. By my faith so will not I, For fear I be outfaced of my way. Well i'll go arm myself, hay. The Duke of Bourbon longs for morning I he longs to eat the English. I think he'll eat all he kills. O peace, ill will never said well. I'll cap that proverb, With there is flattery in friendship. O sir, I can answer that, With give the devil his due. Have at the eye of that proverb, With a jog of the devil. Well the Duke of Bourbon, is simply, The most active Gentleman of France. Doing his activity, and he'll still be doing. He never did hurt as I heard off. No I warrant you, nor never will. I hold him to be exceeding valiant. I was told so by one that knows him better than you. Whos's that? Why he told me so himself: And said he cared not who knew it. Well who will go with me to hazard, For a hundred English prisoners? You must go to hazard yourself, Before you have them. My Lords, the English lie within a hundred Paces of your Tent. Who hath measured the ground? The Lord Granpeere.
