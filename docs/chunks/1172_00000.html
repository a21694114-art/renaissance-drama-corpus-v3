Text (EarlyPrint-regularized spelling)

What do you lack, Gentlemen? what is't you lack? any fine Fancies, Figures, Humours, Characters, Ideas, Definitions of Lords, and Ladies? Waiting-women, Parasites, Knights, Captains, Courtiers, Lawyers? what do you lack? A pretty prompt Boy for the Poëtique Shop. And a bold! where's one o' your Masters, Sirrah, the Poet? Which of 'hem? Sir we have divers that drive that trade, now: Poets, Poet'accios, Poetasters, Poetitoes— And all Haberdashers of small wit, I presume: we would speak with the Poet o' the day, Boy. Sir, he is not here. But, I have the dominion of the Shop, for this time, under him, and can show you all the variety the Stage will afford for the present. Therein you will express your own good parts, Boy. And tie us two, to you, for the gentle office. We are a pair of public persons (this Gentleman, and myself) that are sent, thus coupled unto you upon state-busines. It concerns but the state of the Stage I hope! O, you shall know that by degrees, No man leaps into a business of state, without fourding first the state of the business. We are sent unto you, indeed from the people. The people! which side of the people? The Venison side, if you know it, Boy. That's the left side. I had rather they had been the right. So they are. Not the Paces, or grounds of your people, that 〈◊〉 in the obliqne caves and wedges of your house, your sinful sixpenny Mechanics— But the better, and braver sort of your people! Plush and Velvet-outsides! that stick your house round like so many eminences— Of clothes, not understandings? They are at pawn. Well, I take these as a part of your people though; what bring you to me from these people? You have heard, Boy, the ancient Poets had it in their purpose, still to please this people. I, their chief aim was— Populo ut placerent: (if he understands so much.) Quas fecissent fabulas.) I understand that, sin ' I learned Terence, i'the third form at Westminster: go on Sir. Now, these people have employed us to you, in all their names, to entreat an excellent Play from you. For they have had very mean ones, from this shop of late, the Stage as you call it. Troth, Gentlemen, I have no wares, which I dare thrust upon the people with praise. But this, such as it is, I will venture with your people, your gay gallant people: so as you, again, will undertake for them, that they shall know a good Play when they hear it; and will have the conscience, and ingenuity beside, to confess it. we'll pass our words for that: you shall have a brace of us to engage ourselves. You'll tender your names, Gentlemen, to our book than? Yes, here's Mr. Probee; A man of most powerful speech, and parts to persuade. And Mr. Damplay, will make good all he undertakes. Good Mr. Probee, and Mr. Damplay! I like your securities: whence do you writ yourselves?
